# The Home Design Playbook

Oct 7, 2026 · @Jonathan Chen

## The mindset

Good design is a set of decisions made in the right order, for how you actually live. Taste matters, but most rooms that feel off fail on scale, light, or circulation, not style.

The philosophies pros actually work from:

- **Function first, then form.** Write down what happens in each room (who, when, doing what) before picking a single object. A gorgeous living room with no place to set a drink is a failed room.
- **Design for the 95%, not the 5%.** Don't build the dining room around Thanksgiving if you eat there four times a year. Make it flex instead.
- **Edit, then edit again.** The most common amateur tell is too much stuff at the same visual weight. Pros remove one more thing before calling it done.
- **Every room needs tension.** Old with new, rough with smooth, curved with straight, expensive with humble. All-matching reads like a showroom. All-contrast reads like chaos. The sweet spot is about 80% calm, 20% surprise.
- **Light is a material.** You're designing for daylight at 9am, 4pm, and lamplight at 9pm. Test everything in all three.
- **Buy fewer, better.** Spend on what you touch daily (sofa, mattress, desk chair, faucets, door hardware). Save on what you only look at.
- **A home should look collected, not bought.** If everything arrives in one month from one store, it shows. Leave room to layer in over years.
- **Respect the architecture.** Work with the house's bones, ceiling height, windows, and era. Fighting the architecture is expensive and rarely wins.

The philosophies themselves (for vocabulary when you're browsing or briefing a designer): Modern, Mid-Century Modern, Scandinavian, Japandi, Minimalist, Transitional, Traditional, Modern Farmhouse, Industrial, Organic Modern, Warm Minimalism, Wabi-sabi, Art Deco, Maximalism, Coastal, Bohemian, Mediterranean, and Contemporary (which just means "what's current"). Most great homes are a deliberate blend of two, with one leading.

## The basics

Six principles explain why a room works. Learn to diagnose with them and you can fix almost any space.

1. **Scale and proportion.** Scale is how big a thing is relative to the room; proportion is how parts relate to each other. The #1 mistake is everything too small: tiny rugs, art floating on huge walls, skinny curtains, pendants that look like ornaments. When in doubt, go bigger and use fewer pieces.
2. **Balance.** Symmetrical (formal, calming: matching lamps, centered sofa), asymmetrical (relaxed, more interesting: a big chair balanced by a tall plant and a stack of books), or radial (around a center, like a round table). Balance visual weight, not just object count. Dark, textured, and bulky things weigh more.
3. **Focal point.** Every room gets one main thing the eye lands on: fireplace, view, art, statement bed. One. Two competing focal points (huge TV next to a fireplace with art over it) is the classic fight.
4. **Rhythm.** Repeat a color, shape, or material three or more times around a room so the eye travels. A brass lamp, brass picture frame, brass drawer pulls. Odd numbers (3, 5) read more natural than pairs in styled groupings.
5. **Contrast and variety.** Mix shapes (round coffee table with a boxy sofa), heights (low, mid, tall in every room), and values (lights and darks). A room where everything sits at 30 inches tall is flat and boring.
6. **Circulation.** People should walk through a room without weaving. Plan the paths first, then put furniture around them, never in them.

**Visual weight cheat:** legs make furniture feel lighter (great in small rooms), skirted or blocky pieces feel heavier. Glass and lucite disappear. Dark colors recede on walls but feel heavy in objects.

## Color

Color is mostly about undertone and light, not the name on the chip. Get those two right and almost any palette works.

**The 60-30-10 rule.** 60% dominant (walls, big floor area), 30% secondary (furniture, rugs, curtains), 10% accent (pillows, art, a lamp, a vase). It's a starting point, not law, but it stops the "every pillow is a different color" problem.

**Undertones are everything.** Every white, gray, and beige leans warm (yellow, red, pink) or cool (blue, green, violet). Clashing undertones are why a "neutral" room looks dirty. Hold chips against a sheet of pure white printer paper to see the lean. Match the undertone of your fixed elements (floors, countertops, tile, brick) first, since you can't easily change those.

**Light direction changes paint.**

| Window faces | Light quality | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| North | Cool, flat, bluish all day | Use warm undertones; avoid cool grays, they go gloomy |
| South | Warm, bright, strong | Cooler or crisp colors handle it; warm colors can get intense |
| East | Warm morning, cool afternoon | Test at both times |
| West | Cool morning, golden evening | Test at both times; warm colors glow at sunset |

**Testing.** Never pick from a 1-inch chip. Get 2x2 ft peel-and-stick samples (Samplize) or paint poster boards, move them around the room, and look at morning, afternoon, and night under your actual bulbs. Colors look roughly two shades darker and more saturated on a full wall.

**LRV (Light Reflectance Value).** 0 is black, 100 is pure white. Most "go-to" walls sit 60 to 85. Below 50 reads as a real color commitment. Dark rooms with little light: don't just go lighter, a pale color in a dark room just looks gray. Sometimes going moody and dark is the better move.

**Sheen (finish).** Flat/matte hides wall flaws, best for ceilings and low-traffic rooms. Eggshell or matte-washable for most walls. Satin for kids' rooms, bathrooms, hallways. Semi-gloss for trim and doors. High gloss is a design statement (lacquered rooms) and shows every flaw.

**Pro moves:**

- Paint ceilings a slightly lighter version of the wall, or the same color at 50% strength, instead of default bright white. It makes the room feel finished.
- Color drenching: walls, trim, and ceiling all one color. Looks expensive and makes small rooms feel bigger and cozier.
- Pick your trim white once and use it everywhere. Mixing whites is a common mess.
- Use a whole-house palette (see the cohesion section): a core neutral, plus 3 to 5 colors that rotate as accents room to room.
- Wood tones count as colors. Don't match them exactly; mix 2 to 3 wood tones with a shared undertone.
- Black is a neutral. A little black (frames, a lamp base, window mullions) grounds a room.

## Lighting

Lighting is the single biggest upgrade most homes need, and the one most people skip. A $50k living room under one overhead fixture looks worse than a $5k room with good layered light.

**Three layers in every room:**

- **Ambient:** general fill (recessed cans, flush mounts, cove lighting).
- **Task:** where you do things (reading lamps, under-cabinet kitchen strips, vanity lights, desk lamps).
- **Accent:** drama and depth (picture lights, uplights on plants, lit shelves, wall washers on texture).

Pro target: at least 3 to 5 light sources in a living room, at different heights, most of them below eye level in the evening. Overhead-only light flattens faces and makes rooms feel like offices.

**Color temperature (Kelvin):**

| Kelvin | Feel | Use for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2200 to 2700K | Candle-warm, cozy | Living, bedrooms, dining, evenings |
| 3000K | Warm-neutral, clean | Kitchens, baths, whole-house default if you want one number |
| 3500 to 4000K | Neutral to cool | Garages, workshops, closets for color matching |
| 5000K+ | Daylight-blue | Almost never in a home. Feels clinical |

Rule: never mix color temperatures in the same room or sightline. One 4000K bulb in a 2700K room looks broken.

**CRI (Color Rendering Index).** Buy 90+ CRI, ideally 95+. Cheap LEDs (80 CRI) make skin, wood, and food look gray and sickly. This is a hidden reason expensive homes look better.

**Dimmers on everything.** Every circuit you can. Use LED-compatible dimmers and confirm the fixtures dim smoothly to low levels. Warm-dim ("dim-to-warm") fixtures shift from 3000K to about 1800K as they dim, like incandescent did. Gorgeous in dining and living rooms.

**Recessed lighting gotchas:**

- Don't grid the ceiling like an airport ("pothole ceiling"). Place cans to light walls and work surfaces, not the middle of the floor.
- Keep wall-wash cans about 18 to 36 inches off the wall depending on ceiling height.
- Smaller apertures (2 to 4 inch) and deep-regressed trims reduce glare and look more refined.
- In kitchens, place cans over the front edge of the counter, not behind you, or you'll work in your own shadow.

**Sizing fixtures:**

- **Chandelier diameter (inches)** ≈ room length + width in feet. A 12 x 14 ft room → about 26 inch chandelier.
- **Over a dining table:** fixture width about 1/2 to 2/3 of the table width; bottom 30 to 36 inches above the tabletop (add \~3 inches per foot of ceiling above 8 ft).
- **Kitchen island pendants:** 30 to 36 inches above the counter; leave 6 inches or more from each end; 2 pendants for islands up to \~7 ft, 3 for longer.
- **Entry/foyer:** bottom of the fixture at least 7 ft above the floor. In two-story foyers, hang it roughly at second-floor level.
- **Bathroom vanity:** sconces at eye level (about 60 to 66 inches) on either side of the mirror beat a single bar above it, which casts shadows under your eyes.
- **Table lamps:** the bottom of the shade should land at about eye level when seated (roughly 38 to 42 inches from floor), so you see light, not bulb.

**Wiring decisions to make before walls close:** switch locations (by every door, at the top and bottom of stairs), floor outlets under sofas that float off walls, outlets inside cabinets and drawers for charging, picture light junction boxes over art, wiring for sconces beside beds, and a smart lighting system (Lutron is the standard) if you want scenes.

## The numbers cheat sheet

Hang art with its center at 57 to 60 inches off the floor, the average human eye level used by galleries. Most people hang art too high. Here's every number worth memorizing.

**Art and mirrors**

| Situation | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Art on an open wall | Center at 57 to 60 in. from floor |
| Art over a sofa, console, or bed headboard | Bottom edge 6 to 10 in. above the furniture top |
| Width of art over furniture | About 2/3 to 3/4 of the furniture's width |
| Gallery wall spacing | 2 to 3 in. between frames, consistently; treat the whole group as one piece centered at 57 to 60 in. |
| Art over a fireplace mantel | Bottom 4 to 8 in. above mantel; can lean art on the mantel for a relaxed look |
| Tall ceilings (10 ft+) | Still about eye level. Scale the art up instead of hanging higher |
| Mirror over a vanity | Slightly narrower than the vanity; top roughly aligned with sconces or door trim height |

**Rugs**

| Room | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Living room | At least the front legs of every seat on the rug; best is all legs. Typically 8x10 or 9x12, rarely 5x8 |
| Rug to wall gap | Leave 8 to 18 in. of floor visible around the edge |
| Dining room | Rug extends 24 in. or more past the table on every side so chairs stay on it when pulled out |
| Bedroom (queen) | 8x10 placed under the lower 2/3 of the bed, extending 18 to 24 in. past the sides |
| Bedroom (king) | 9x12 or 10x14 |
| Hallway runner | Leave 4 to 6 in. of floor on each side and a few inches at the ends |

**Clearances and walkways**

| Space | Dimension |
| --- | --- |
| Main walkway | 36 in. minimum, 42 to 48 in. ideal |
| Coffee table to sofa | 14 to 18 in. |
| Coffee table length | About 2/3 the sofa length; height within 1 to 2 in. of seat height |
| Side table height | Within 2 in. of the sofa or chair arm |
| Sofa to TV | About 1.5 to 2.5 x the screen diagonal; center of screen near seated eye level (\~42 in.), not over the fireplace if you can avoid it |
| Conversation zone | Seats no more than 8 to 10 ft apart |
| Dining chair pull-out | 36 in. from table edge to wall; 42 to 48 in. if people walk behind |
| Dining table per person | 24 in. width per person, 30 in. for comfort |
| Table height / seat height | 30 in. table, 18 in. seat; 12 in. gap from seat to tabletop |
| Kitchen island to counter | 42 in. one cook, 48 in. two cooks |
| Island overhang for seating | 12 in. at counter height (36 in.), 15 in. or more for knees |
| Counter vs bar stool | 24 to 26 in. stool for 36 in. counter; 28 to 30 in. stool for 42 in. bar |
| Bed side clearance | 24 in. min, 30 to 36 in. ideal |
| Nightstand height | Level with or slightly above mattress top |
| Toilet centerline | 15 in. min to side wall (18 in. better), 21 in. min clear in front (30 in. better) |
| Desk height | 28 to 30 in.; keyboard tray lower is fine |

**Windows and curtains**

- Hang rods high and wide: 4 to 6 in. below the ceiling (or halfway between window top and ceiling at minimum), extending 8 to 12 in. past each side of the frame so open panels don't block glass.
- Panels should kiss the floor or break by half an inch. Never "high-water" curtains that stop above the floor.
- Fullness: total fabric width 2 to 2.5x the window width. Skimpy curtains are a top amateur tell.
- Linen and performance blends look more expensive than shiny polyester. Lined panels hang better.

**Shelving and other**

- Floating shelf spacing: 12 to 15 in. vertically for books and objects.
- Kitchen upper cabinets: 18 in. above the counter.
- Towel bars: about 48 in. high. Robe hooks: 70 in.
- Pendant in a hallway or open space: 7 ft minimum clearance.

## Materials, texture, and finishes

Texture is what makes a neutral room look rich instead of bland. If a room feels flat but you like the colors, the fix is almost always more texture, not more color.

**Layer at least 4 to 5 textures per room:** something nubby (boucle, wool), something smooth (leather, lacquer, stone), something natural (wood, rattan, linen, jute), something with sheen (metal, glass, ceramic glaze), and something living (plants, flowers, branches).

**Metals.** Mixing is fine and looks better than matching everything. Pick one dominant metal (about 70%) and one or two supporting ones. Keep plumbing fixtures in a single room consistent. Living finishes (unlacquered brass, aged bronze) patina over time and look better with age. Polished chrome is the cleanest, but trends cold.

**Wood.** Mix 2 to 3 tones max per room, with a shared undertone, and vary them clearly (a light oak floor with a dark walnut table reads intentional; two almost-matching browns reads like a mistake). Real wood veneer on good substrate is fine and often more stable than solid wood for cabinets.

**Stone and counters.**

| Material | Pros | Watch out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quartzite | Natural stone, very hard, looks like marble | Some "soft quartzite" is actually marble. Do the etch test with lemon juice on a sample |
| Marble | Most beautiful, timeless | Etches from acid and stains. Choose honed over polished to hide etching. Embrace patina or don't buy it |
| Quartz (engineered) | Consistent, low maintenance | Can look fake in fake-marble patterns; not great near heat; silica fabrication concerns |
| Granite | Durable, cheap | Busy speckled slabs date fast. Leathered or honed finishes look modern |
| Soapstone | Soft, warm, historic | Scratches (sand out); darkens with oil |
| Porcelain slab | Thin, heat-resistant, outdoor-safe | Pattern is printed on top; chipped edges show the body |

Always pick your actual slab in person at the yard. Two slabs of the same name can look completely different.

**Tile.** Fewer tile types is more elegant: 2 to 3 per bathroom max. Large format tiles make small rooms feel bigger. Grout color matters as much as the tile: matching grout reads calm, contrasting grout highlights pattern (and dirt). Run floor tile into the shower when possible for a seamless look.

**Fabrics.** Performance fabrics (Crypton, Sunbrella, performance velvet) for anything kids, pets, or red wine touch. Check the double rub count: 15,000+ for daily use, 30,000+ for heavy use. Linen wrinkles (that's the point). Velvet crushes. Boucle pills over time, especially cheap ones.

**Flooring.** Run the same flooring continuously across connected spaces. Changing flooring at every doorway chops up a home. Wide plank (7 to 9 in.), matte finish, and natural oak tones are the current "expensive" look. Engineered hardwood is better than solid on concrete or in humid areas.

**Hardware and the touch points.** Door hardware, cabinet pulls, light switches, and outlets are touched constantly and seen in every room. Upgrading them (solid brass levers, screwless plates, decorator switches) is a small cost that changes how a whole house feels.

## Whole-home cohesion vs room themes

The answer is neither. The best homes share one backbone everywhere and let each room have its own personality on top of it. Think of it like a band: same musicians, different songs.

**Why "everything matches" fails:** it feels like a hotel or model home. No story, no discovery, nothing to look at twice. It also ages all at once, because one trend dates the whole house together.

**Why "every room its own theme" fails:** walking from a coastal bedroom to an industrial kitchen to a Moroccan den feels like a furniture showroom. In open plans it's worse, because you see three "themes" at once. Themed rooms (nautical, safari, Paris) also date fast and read juvenile. Avoid theme rooms, period. Use moods instead.

**What stays consistent (the backbone):**

- Flooring, running continuously wherever possible.
- Trim and door color, door style, and door hardware finish.
- One trim white and one ceiling approach.
- A whole-house palette: one core neutral plus 3 to 5 accent colors that show up in different proportions room to room.
- Overall architectural language (modern, traditional, transitional) and level of formality.
- Light color temperature.

**What changes room to room (the personality):**

- Which accent color leads (blue-led study, green-led dining, warm rust in the den).
- Wall treatment intensity: one room drenched in a deep color, one wallpapered, most calm.
- Mood: the bedroom quieter, the dining room more dramatic, the powder room the boldest room in the house (it's small, low-commitment, and guests remember it).
- Furniture mix and the specific "surprise" piece.

**The sightline test.** Stand in any spot and look at every room you can see at once. Those rooms must feel related. Rooms behind closed doors (bedrooms, powder room, office) can go further on their own.

**How to "spice it up" without breaking cohesion:** put about 80% of each room within the core style and 20% outside it. A vintage rug in a modern room. A sculptural modern chair in a traditional room. One antique per room. An unexpected light fixture. A bold ceiling. That 20% is what makes a home look designed by a person instead of a catalog.

**Transitions:** carry at least one element from each room into the next (a color, a wood tone, a metal) so the eye has a thread to follow.

## Custom built-ins vs buying furniture

Build in when the piece solves an awkward space or is part of the architecture; buy when it's something you might move, replace, or take with you. Built-ins add value to the house, furniture adds value to your life.

**Go custom when:**

- The space is awkward: alcoves, under stairs, sloped ceilings, odd niches, around windows, flanking a fireplace. Store-bought furniture leaves dead gaps that look cheap.
- You need wall-to-wall or floor-to-ceiling storage (libraries, media walls, mudrooms, closets, pantries). Floor to ceiling is where custom crushes buying.
- The piece is architectural: window seats, banquettes, paneling, kitchen and bath cabinetry, a bar, a desk nook.
- Storage density matters. Custom can use every inch, including the top 18 inches most furniture wastes.
- You'll be in the home 7+ years.

**Buy when:**

- It's something you sit or sleep on. Sofas, chairs, and beds need to be tested with your body. Custom upholstery from a real workroom is the exception.
- You might move within \~5 years, or the room's use may change (a nursery that becomes an office).
- It's the "soul" layer: vintage and antique pieces. These are what keep a home from looking new and builder-grade.
- You want flexibility to rearrange, or the piece is the 20% surprise element.

**The middle ground (often the smart answer):**

- **Semi-custom:** IKEA or other stock cabinet boxes with custom fronts (Semihandmade, Reform, Plykea) and a carpenter adding fillers, crown, and baseboard so it looks built in. Often 40 to 60% of full custom.
- **Furniture that looks built in:** a run of matching bookcases scribed to the wall and trimmed out.
- **Custom-sized but freestanding:** a local maker builds a dining table or credenza to your exact dimensions; it still moves with you.

**Built-in gotchas:**

- Plan power, lighting, and data before building: outlets inside cabinets, LED strip channels, TV wiring, speaker wire, ventilation for AV gear.
- Media walls: design around the TV size you'll have in 5 years, not the current one, or build adjustable. TVs date faster than cabinetry.
- Depth: bookshelves 12 to 14 in. deep; anything deeper than 16 in. becomes a junk cave. Base cabinets 18 to 24 in.
- Over-trendy built-ins (heavy color, of-the-moment profiles) are hard to undo. Keep the bones classic and add trend through paint and hardware.
- Always shop drawings before fabrication, and check the finish samples in your actual room light.
- Custom takes 8 to 20 weeks. Order early.

## Advanced concepts

These are the moves that separate "nice" from "designed." Most are invisible to guests; they just feel that the house is right.

- **Sightlines and the axis.** From every doorway, what's the first thing you see? Put something worth seeing at the end of every long view: art, a lit console, a window, a great chair. Never a toilet, laundry, or the back of a TV.
- **Compression and release.** A lower, darker, narrower space (entry hall) makes the next big bright room feel more dramatic. Use it intentionally rather than fighting small spaces.
- **Zoning open plans.** Define zones with rugs, lighting (a fixture over each zone), furniture backs, and ceiling changes, not walls. Each zone needs its own anchor.
- **Float the furniture.** Pulling sofas off walls into conversation groups makes rooms feel bigger and more intentional. Pushing everything against the walls is the dorm-room default.
- **Layering in depth.** Good rooms have foreground, middle, and background: a chair in front, a table, art behind. Same principle as photography.
- **The fifth wall.** Ceilings: paint, wallpaper, wood, beams, coffers, or just a tint of the wall color. Most homes ignore them entirely.
- **Negative space.** Empty wall and empty floor are design elements. Not every surface needs an object. Gives important pieces room to matter.
- **Highs and lows.** Mix a $40 vintage find next to a $4,000 chair. Rooms where everything is the same price point (high or low) feel flat.
- **Patina and provenance.** At least one old thing per room. Age gives depth that new things can't fake.
- **Pattern mixing.** Use three scales: one large, one medium, one small, tied by a shared color. Vary the pattern type (floral, stripe, geometric). Solids give the eye a rest.
- **Lines.** Horizontal lines calm and widen (long low sofas, shelves). Vertical lines lift (tall drapes, paneling, tall art). Curves soften and are the antidote to a boxy room.
- **Window treatments as architecture.** Drapes hung high and wide fix bad window proportions and make ceilings read taller.
- **Acoustics.** Hard modern homes echo. Rugs, upholstery, drapes, books, and acoustic panels or felt behind art make a home feel calmer than you'd guess.
- **Mock up before committing.** Tape outlines on the floor for furniture, cardboard boxes for volume, blue tape on walls for art and built-ins. Live with it for a couple of days.
- **Hide the ugly stuff.** Routers, cords, thermostats, vents, sensors, speakers, and smart-home hubs. Plan homes for them so they don't land on your best wall.
- **Biophilic design.** Plants, natural materials, views, and daylight measurably make people feel better. Real plants beat faux, but good faux beats dead real.
- **Senses beyond sight.** Scent, temperature, sound, and touch. A great home smells like something, sounds quiet, and feels good underfoot.
- **Styling formulas.** Coffee table: a stack of books, one natural element, one sculptural object, a tray to corral it. Shelves: mix vertical books, horizontal stacks, objects, and empty space, in a zig-zag pattern across shelves. Bed: two sleeping pillows, two euros, one lumbar or two accents, max.

## Room by room

Each room has a job; design for that job first and the style follows.

| Room | What matters most | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entry | Drop zone (keys, shoes, bags), a mirror, a lamp, durable floor, a bench | No storage, so clutter migrates into the house |
| Living room | Conversation layout, layered light, big enough rug, comfortable seating at the right depth | TV dictating everything; furniture pushed to walls |
| Kitchen | Work triangle or zones (prep, cook, clean), 36 to 42 in. of prep space, good task lighting, storage over open shelving | Too much open shelving; trendy cabinet color in a hard-to-change material |
| Dining | Table size for daily use with an extension, comfortable chairs, a dimmable fixture at the right height | Rug too small; chandelier too high or too small |
| Primary bedroom | Calm palette, blackout plus sheer layers, bedside reading light, nightstands at mattress height, no work stuff | TV wall dominating; overhead-only light |
| Bathrooms | Ventilation, sconces at face height, niches for products, heated floors if possible, enough storage | Too many tile types; single light above the mirror |
| Powder room | The boldest room in the house: wallpaper, dark color, statement mirror and faucet | Playing it safe in the one room where you shouldn't |
| Office | Desk facing a view or into the room (not a wall), camera-friendly background, closed storage, a good chair | Backlit by a window on video calls |
| Kids' rooms | Durable finishes, grow-with-them furniture, low storage they can reach | Themed decor that's outgrown in 2 years |
| Gym | Rubber flooring, mirrors, ventilation, a mounted TV, outlets, sound isolation if upstairs | Weak floor structure under heavy equipment |
| Laundry / mudroom | Lockers or cubbies, a bench, hooks, a sink, a folding counter | Forgotten as a design space entirely |

**Bedroom specifics:** the bed is the focal point, so give it a strong headboard or wall treatment. Lamps on both sides, even if one person reads. Sconces on swing arms free up nightstand space.

**TV strategy:** the TV is a black rectangle, so either embrace it (dedicated media wall with built-ins and dark paint so it disappears) or hide it (Frame TV, cabinet, or art lift). The worst option is mounting it high over a fireplace; it's a neck strain and it fights the focal point.

## Gotchas and things not to do

Most of these cost money to fix after the fact. Read this list twice.

**Buying and planning**

- Buying furniture before measuring doorways, stairwells, elevators, and turns. Measure the path in, not just the room.
- Picking paint first. Paint is the last decision; it's the cheapest and has the most options. Pick fabrics, rugs, and art first, then pull paint from them.
- Buying all matching sets (bedroom set, living room set). Instant catalog look.
- Buying everything at once from one retailer.
- Rushing to fill a room. An empty corner is better than the wrong chair. Live in the space first.
- Ignoring lead times. Custom upholstery 10 to 16 weeks, lighting and tile 4 to 12 weeks. Order long-lead items first.
- Not checking return policies. Many custom, clearance, and large items are final sale.

**Scale and placement**

- Rugs too small ("floating postage stamp").
- Art too small or hung too high.
- Curtains hung right at the window frame, too short, or too skimpy.
- Furniture too big for the room (oversized sectionals in normal rooms) or too small (apartment-sized pieces in big rooms).
- Every piece the same height.
- Blocking windows, vents, or walkways.

**Light and color**

- One overhead light per room. Or a grid of cans and nothing else.
- Mixing bulb color temperatures.
- Cheap, low-CRI bulbs.
- Choosing paint under store lighting from a tiny chip.
- Cool gray everything (the 2015 look is dating fast).
- "Safe" beige that clashes with the undertone of your floors.

**Style and materials**

- Theme rooms. Literal decor ("Eat" signs, "Live Laugh Love," anchors in a beach house).
- Chasing a trend in an expensive, permanent material. Trends belong in paint, pillows, and accessories.
- Faux everything (faux wood vinyl in luxury spaces, plastic plants, faux marble printed laminates). One good real thing beats five fakes.
- Too many accessories at the same small scale ("tchotchke creep").
- Choosing fabrics that won't survive your life (white linen sofa with a dog).
- Ignoring maintenance: marble, unlacquered brass, and white oak all need care. Decide if you'll actually do it.

**Construction and fixed elements**

- Skipping electrical planning before drywall: outlets, switches, sconces, floor outlets, low-voltage.
- Shallow or flat showers without enough drain slope, or no niche.
- Forgetting blocking in walls for heavy mirrors, TVs, grab bars, floating vanities, and floating shelves.
- Under-sized bathroom exhaust fans (mold).
- Contractor-grade switch plates, door knobs, and outlets left in an otherwise beautiful home.

## Process: the order of decisions

Decide from most permanent to least permanent. Paint is last; walls and wiring are first.

1. **Brief.** How you live: who, routines, entertaining, work-from-home, pets, storage needs, what you hate about your current home.
2. **Inspiration and direction.** Save 30 to 50 images, then find the pattern. Name your two lead styles and the mood of each room in one sentence.
3. **Measure and floor plan.** Measure every room, window, door, outlet, vent, and ceiling height. Draw it to scale (Floorplanner, SketchUp, or graph paper).
4. **Layout and circulation.** Place furniture on the plan; check walkways and sightlines.
5. **Fixed elements.** Electrical, lighting plan, plumbing, built-ins, flooring, tile, counters, cabinetry. This is when blocking and outlets get planned.
6. **Whole-house palette.** Core neutral, trim white, wood tones, metals, and accent colors.
7. **Anchor pieces.** Sofa, bed, dining table, big rugs. The things that set scale.
8. **Lighting fixtures and window treatments.**
9. **Secondary furniture.** Chairs, side tables, consoles, nightstands.
10. **Paint.** Final colors pulled from everything above, tested in place.
11. **Art, accessories, and plants.** Collected over time. This layer never really finishes.

**Budget split rule of thumb** for furnishing (not construction): about 40% on seating, beds, and dining; 20% on lighting and window treatments; 20% on rugs; 20% on art, accessories, and the rest. Keep a 10 to 15% contingency for surprises.

**When to hire help:** a designer pays for themselves on layouts, lighting plans, built-ins, and kitchen/bath selections, where mistakes are expensive and permanent. Many work hourly for a consultation and a plan you execute yourself.
